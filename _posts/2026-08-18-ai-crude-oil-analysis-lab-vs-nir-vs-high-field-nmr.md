  ## Why crude oil analysis method choice matters now

  Crude oil analysis determines how a refinery values, blends, and processes each crude type. Refinery R&D teams must compare methods based on cost, turnaround time, precision, and operational complexity. A method suited to certification may delay routine decisions, while a fast screening method may provide less compositional detail.

  - **Laboratory testing** measures crude properties through established physical and chemical procedures, making it suitable for formal assays and certification.
  - **NIR** estimates properties by matching near-infrared spectra against calibrated reference samples, which supports rapid screening of familiar crude types.
  - **High-Field NMR** produces detailed molecular spectra for deep structural analysis, but specialized instruments and expertise increase operating complexity.
  - **Rombo AI** combines low-field NMR with pretrained machine learning models to convert spectra into operational property estimates. The approach targets routine monitoring across instruments, crude types, and refinery locations.

  The right choice depends on whether you need an authoritative assay, quick screening, detailed molecular analysis, or repeatable operational monitoring.

  ## How the comparison is evaluated

  Each method is evaluated across cost, turnaround time, measurement performance, and operational complexity. Cost includes instrument purchases, external testing fees, calibration, software, and specialist labor. Turnaround runs from sample receipt to a decision-ready result. Measurement performance considers repeatability and agreement with reference assay values across crude types.

  Operational complexity reflects staffing, calibration, sample preparation, and maintenance. Low complexity requires routine operators and limited recalibration. Medium complexity requires trained staff or periodic model updates. High complexity requires specialist interpretation, extensive preparation, or frequent instrument-specific calibration. The comparison reflects refinery operating use rather than regulatory acceptance or research capability.

  ## Cost: lab fees, instrument capex, and per-sample economics

  Laboratory testing creates a substantial recurring expense because each crude type requires a new assay. The reference cost of **about $50,000 per crude type** can include external laboratory fees, sample handling, multiple property tests, and reporting. A refinery that evaluates unfamiliar feedstocks regularly keeps incurring those costs, while repeat tests add further expense.

  NIR replaces much of the external testing expense with instrument ownership and calibration work. Each model needs laboratory reference data that connects measured spectra to properties such as density or sulfur content. Once a model covers a stable crude family, the cost per additional sample can become low. New crude types or changing compositions may require more reference assays and calibration work.

  High-field NMR carries the largest equipment and facility burden of the instrument-based options. Refineries must account for the spectrometer, installation requirements, service contracts, specialist staff, and supporting laboratory infrastructure. High sample volume can spread those fixed costs across more analyses, but low utilization leaves a high effective cost per sample.

  [Rombo AI](https://rombo.ai) shifts more spending toward software and lower-field NMR equipment. Its pretrained models reduce the amount of crude-specific data needed for model development compared with conventional chemometric approaches. Rombo does not publish standard pricing, so buyers should compare software licensing, instrument costs, validation work, and expected sample volume in a total-cost calculation.

  Throughput determines the practical cost winner. Laboratory testing suits occasional certified assays because it avoids instrument ownership. NIR can produce economical screening when crude composition stays within a well-calibrated range. High-field NMR makes financial sense when deep structural information supports enough research work to justify the equipment. Rombo’s approach targets recurring operational analysis where faster model deployment and lower-field hardware can reduce cost across a larger sample load.

  ## Turnaround time: weeks, hours, or minutes

  Traditional laboratory testing can take weeks when a refinery needs a full crude assay. Multiple tests, sample preparation, laboratory queues, and report review extend the sample-to-answer clock. A refinery may need to hold a blending decision or rely on an earlier assay while the new results remain pending.

  NIR produces a spectrum within seconds or minutes, but calibration determines when you receive a usable answer. A crude type outside the calibration set may require reference testing and model updates. Those extra steps can turn a rapid scan into an hours-long or multi-day workflow.

  High-field NMR can acquire detailed spectra within minutes or hours, depending on the experiment. Sample preparation, instrument availability, and specialist interpretation add more time. Recalibration or method changes can create further delays when the crude composition or instrument configuration changes.

  [Rombo AI](https://rombo.ai) reports a sample-to-answer time of about **15 minutes** by combining low-field NMR with a pretrained machine learning model. The model interprets the spectrum without waiting for a conventional laboratory assay or a specialist to review each result manually. A refinery can use that shorter cycle to adjust blending or processing decisions during the same operating window rather than after the crude has already moved through the facility.

  ## Precision and accuracy across crude types

  Rombo AI reports a **2% precision** figure for predicted properties under validated test conditions. Buyers should confirm whether the percentage represents relative error, absolute error, or repeatability for each property. A single percentage cannot describe performance across density, sulfur content, viscosity, and every crude grade equally.

  Traditional laboratory assays remain the reference for certified composition data because each test measures a defined property through an established procedure. Their accuracy depends on sampling, preparation, and the chosen method, but they provide the benchmark used to validate faster techniques.

  NIR estimates crude properties by comparing a sample’s spectrum with calibration data. NIR can produce reliable results for crude types represented in its training set, but accuracy often declines when composition falls outside that range. New crude families may require additional reference samples and recalibration.

  High-Field NMR provides greater raw spectral resolution than low-field instruments, which helps specialists separate overlapping chemical signals. Raw resolution does not automatically produce more accurate operational predictions. Analysts still need to interpret the spectrum and connect its features to the properties a refinery uses.

  [Rombo AI](https://rombo.ai) combines low-field NMR with pretrained machine learning models that interpret spectral patterns and estimate crude properties. The model can extract useful predictions without matching high-field NMR’s raw resolution, but buyers should validate the reported **2% precision** against their own crude slate. Unseen crude types, instrument conditions, and reference data quality can affect model accuracy.

  ## Operational complexity: instruments, staffing, and multi-site rollout

  Laboratory testing requires the most coordination across methods and people. Specialist technicians prepare samples, run separate assays, review results, and maintain quality controls. A multi-site program also needs shared procedures, reference materials, and proficiency testing so each laboratory produces comparable results.

  NIR requires less operator expertise during routine screening, but its calibration models create ongoing work. Each model depends on representative crude samples paired with laboratory reference values. New crude types, instrument changes, and environmental differences can require model updates or calibration transfer before another site can trust the results.

  High-field NMR requires trained spectroscopists and specialized infrastructure. Operators must maintain the instrument, control sample preparation, and interpret detailed spectra. Replicating that capability across facilities can require major equipment purchases, site preparation, method validation, and local expertise.

  [Rombo AI](https://rombo.ai) pairs low-field NMR with pretrained machine learning models that interpret spectra. Its vendor-neutral approach normalizes data across mixed instrument fleets and reduces the need to build a separate chemometric calibration for every instrument or location. Minimal recalibration can shorten deployment at additional sites, although each facility still needs sample handling controls, instrument checks, and procedures for reviewing unusual results.

  For routine operation, the staffing difference centers on where expertise sits. Traditional laboratory testing and high-field NMR place more analytical responsibility with local specialists. NIR shifts much of the work into calibration maintenance. Rombo AI shifts more interpretation into the model, which can support consistent workflows across facilities while keeping technical review available for exceptions.

  Choose high-field NMR when refinery R&D needs detailed structural analysis or maximum spectral resolution. The method suits specialized investigations that justify expensive equipment, skilled operators, and slower interpretation.
